A Companion of the Prophet ﷺ

Nusaybah Bint Kaʿb (RA)

Umm ʿĀmmārah, the Lioness of Uḥud, who stood as a shield before the Messenger ﷺ

In 625 CE, when the Muslim lines broke at Uḥud, a woman of Madinah picked up a sword and stood between the Prophet ﷺ and his enemies.

Who Was Nusaybah?

Nusaybah bint Kaʿb al-Māziniyyah (RA) belonged to the Banu Najjār of the Ansar in Madinah and is best known as Umm ʿĀmmārah. She was among the first believers of Yathrib, and her life touched nearly every turning point of the Prophet’s ﷺ Madinan years, from the secret pledge at ʿAqabah to the wars that followed his passing. The sources remember her as a mother, a nurse, a fighter and a narrator of ḥadīth, and above all as a believer who treated the defence of the Messenger ﷺ as a duty, not an option.

Family and Lineage

Her full name was Nusaybah bint Kaʿb ibn ʿAmr ibn ʿAwf, of the Banū Māzin ibn al-Najjār, a clan of the Khazraj in Madinah. The Banū al-Najjār were the Prophet’s ﷺ maternal relatives: his great-grandmother Salmā came from the clan, and he stayed among them when he first reached the city.

Nusaybah married Zayd ibn ʿĀṣim, and they had two sons, ʿAbdullah and Ḥabīb. After Zayd she married Ghaziyyah ibn ʿAmr and had further children. Her kunya, Umm ʿĀmmārah (“mother of ʿĀmmārah”), comes from her son ʿĀmmārah, a form of address that Arabs of the time used as a mark of respect.

Her brothers were also counted among the early Ansar. ʿAbdullah ibn Kaʿb is named among the veterans of Badr, and ʿAbd al-Raḥmān ibn Kaʿb is remembered among the “weepers” (al-bakkāʾūn), the believers who wept because they had no mounts to join the army marching to Tabūk. The early biographers record that her whole household took the Prophet’s ﷺ side.

Early Life and Her Road to Islam

Nusaybah grew up in Yathrib in the years before the Hijrah, in a city divided between the Aws and the Khazraj and worn down by long feuds, the last of them the Battle of Buʿāth (around 617 CE). Islam reached the city through Khazrajī pilgrims who met the Prophet ﷺ at Mina in 620 CE, and after the first pledge of ʿAqabah the following year he sent Muṣʿab ibn ʿUmayr (RA) to teach them. Muṣʿab lodged with Asʿad ibn Zurārah, a man of the Najjār clan, and the households of the Banu al-Najjār, hers included, are understood to have embraced Islam through that teaching.

When the Prophet ﷺ arrived in 622 CE, his camel came to rest in the Najjār quarter, and he stayed in the house of Abū Ayyūb al-Anṣārī (RA) while the mosque was built. Nusaybah therefore lived in the very neighbourhood where the first Muslim community of Madinah took shape.

The Pledge of ʿAqabah

Nusaybah is counted among the two women who travelled from Madinah to Mecca for the second pledge of ʿAqabah, where the Ansar swore to protect the Prophet ﷺ as they would protect their own families. She gave that promise with her own voice, and she would keep it with her own sword.

The pledge took place at ʿAqabah, near Mina, in the pilgrimage season of 622 CE, a few months before the Hijrah. About seventy-three men and two women from Yathrib slipped out of their camp by night, evaded the Quraysh, and met the Prophet ﷺ in secret. His uncle al-ʿAbbās, who had not yet accepted Islam, attended to be sure they understood the danger they were taking on. Al-Barāʾ ibn Maʿrūr spoke for the Ansar, and the Prophet ﷺ then appointed twelve leaders (nuqabāʾ) from among them. The two women were Nusaybah and Asmāʾ bint ʿAmr, and both gave the same pledge to shelter the Messenger ﷺ at the cost of their own safety, in a city that was about to go to war to protect him.

A LATER REVELATION ON WOMEN’S PLEDGE — SŪRAH AL-MUMTAḤANAH 60:12 (MEANING)

“O Prophet, when believing women come to you pledging that they will not associate anything with Allah… then accept their pledge and ask Allah to forgive them.”

This verse was revealed years after ʿAqabah, around the time of Ḥudaybiyyah, so it is not about the pledge at Mina itself. It is quoted here because it describes how the Prophet ﷺ received the pledge of believing women. ʿĀʾishah (RA) explained that he took it by spoken words alone, and his hand never touched the hand of any woman who was not his own (Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī). Nusaybah and Asmāʾ are understood to have pledged by their word in the same manner.

Her Courage at Uḥud

The battle took place at the foot of Mount Uḥud, north of Madinah, in Shawwāl, 3 AH (March 625 CE). A Qurayshī army of about three thousand marched on Madinah to avenge Badr. The Prophet ﷺ placed fifty archers on the hill behind the Muslim lines and ordered them not to leave it whatever happened. When the Muslims seemed to have won, most of the archers left their post for the spoils. Khālid ibn al-Walīd led the cavalry around the hill and struck the Muslims from behind, and the army scattered. The Qurʾān speaks of this moment directly in Sūrah Āl ʿImrān 3:152–153.

Nusaybah had gone out to the field with a water skin, as many Madinan women did, to carry water and tend the wounded. When the Muslims broke, she saw the Prophet ﷺ standing with only a few men around him. She dropped the skin, took up a sword and shield, and moved forward. The early biographers (al-Wāqidī, Ibn Saʿd) report that she fought the enemy’s horsemen on foot and shot arrows with a bow until she had fought her way to the Messenger’s ﷺ side.

The enemy fighter ʿAmr ibn Qamiʾah rode at the Prophet ﷺ, and Nusaybah met him. He struck her on the shoulder, leaving a deep wound that reports say needed a year to heal. She struck back at him with her sword, but he wore two coats of armour and survived. In all, the different reports give her twelve or thirteen wounds that day.

Her son ʿAbdullah ibn Zayd was wounded in the arm, and she bound the wound with a cloth she carried and said, “Get up, my son, and fight the people.” The Prophet ﷺ is reported to have said, “Who can bear what you bear, Umm ʿĀmmārah?” When her son struck down the man who had wounded him, the Prophet ﷺ smiled and said that Allah had given her what she wanted.

A ṣaḥīḥ report gives the context for her stand. Anas (RA) said that when the Muslims scattered, the Prophet ﷺ was left with seven of the Ansar and two of Quraysh. He asked who would drive the enemy off, promising that man Paradise or that he would be his companion in Paradise. One Ansārī stepped forward and was killed, then another, until all seven had fallen (Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim, Kitāb al-Jihād). Nusaybah’s stand belongs to this same defence.

She did not stand alone. The early biographers report that her husband Zayd and both of her sons, ʿAbdullah and Ḥabīb, fought at Uḥud as well, so a whole household was defending the Prophet ﷺ that day. Around them, others held the same line: Abū Ṭalḥah (RA) stood in front of the Prophet ﷺ with his shield and shot arrows over him, and Anas reports in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī that Abū Ṭalḥah broke two or three bows that day (Kitāb al-Jihād).

Her service did not end with the battle. Reports say that on the next morning, when the Prophet ﷺ marched out to Ḥamrāʾ al-Asad to pursue the Quraysh, she tied her waist-wrapper to go with the army but could not, because her wound had drained her strength. After he returned he asked after her and was glad to hear she was alive.

What the Prophet ﷺ Said

The early sources record the Prophet’s ﷺ own testimony to her courage that day, and they record her request to him, which is among the most quoted lines in the Seerah.

REPORTED IN THE EARLY SOURCES OF THE SEERAH

“Wherever I turned, left or right, on the Day of Uḥud, I saw her fighting for me.”

REPORTED BY IBN HISHĀM, AL-WĀQIDĪ AND IBN SAʿD

Umm ʿĀmmārah said: “Messenger of Allah, ask Allah that we be your companions in Paradise.” He said: “O Allah, make them my companions in Paradise.” She replied: “I no longer care what befalls me in this world.”

Her request echoes a promise in the Qurʾān. Sūrah al-Nisāʾ 4:69 says that whoever obeys Allah and the Messenger will be with those Allah has blessed: the prophets, the truthful, the martyrs and the righteous, and what excellent companions they are. The Prophet ﷺ also said, in a ḥadīth narrated by Anas in Bukhārī and Muslim, that a person will be with those he loves.

Ḥudaybiyyah, Ḥunayn and Yamāmah

Uḥud was not her only battlefield. Ibn Saʿd lists her among those present at Ḥudaybiyyah, Khaybar, the ʿUmrah of fulfilment and Ḥunayn during the Prophet’s ﷺ lifetime, and at Yamāmah after his passing.

Ḥudaybiyyah (6 AH). She is reported to have taken part in the pledge of Riḍwān, sworn by some fourteen hundred believers under a tree on the road to Makkah. The Qurʾān records it in Sūrah al-Fatḥ 48:18, where Allah says He was pleased with the believers who pledged to the Prophet ﷺ under the tree.

Khaybar and the ʿUmrah of fulfilment (7 AH). She accompanied the army to Khaybar, the fortified oasis north of Madinah, and later that year she was among the Muslims who entered Makkah for the pilgrimage that the Quraysh had agreed to allow under the Treaty of Ḥudaybiyyah.

Ḥunayn (8 AH). After the conquest of Makkah, about twelve thousand Muslims marched against the tribes of Hawāzin and Thaqīf. The enemy archers ambushed them in a narrow valley, and many of the Muslims scattered. The Prophet ﷺ held his ground with a small group, and al-Barāʾ ibn ʿĀzib (RA) describes in Bukhārī how he stayed on his mule and called out his name and lineage. Sūrah al-Tawbah 9:25–26 says that their large numbers did not help them, until Allah sent down His tranquillity upon the Messenger and the believers. Nusaybah is recorded among those who stood firm there.

Yamāmah (12 AH). Her son Ḥabīb ibn Zayd had been sent as an envoy to Musaylimah, the false prophet of the Banū Ḥanīfah. He refused to accept Musaylimah’s claim, and Musaylimah had him cut to pieces. In 633 CE, when Abū Bakr (RA) sent an army against Musaylimah under Khālid ibn al-Walīd (RA), Nusaybah asked to go to avenge her son. Reports say Abū Bakr, who knew her record, did not refuse her. The fighting was fierce, and the final stand took place in a walled orchard later called the Garden of Death, where Musaylimah was killed, reportedly by Waḥshī and an Anṣārī. Her son ʿAbdullah is also reported to have taken part in killing him. Nusaybah fought through the enemy ranks towards Musaylimah, lost a hand and received eleven more wounds, then returned to Madinah and lived on. The exact year of her death is not firmly recorded.

A narrator of ḥadīth. Nusaybah is also counted among the women who transmitted ḥadīth. She reported that the Prophet ﷺ once visited her and she offered him food. When she said she was fasting, he told her that the angels send blessings on a fasting person while others eat in his presence. This is recorded by al-Tirmidhī, who graded it ḥasan, and by Ibn Mājah.

Her Contribution

  1. A witness across an era. She was a grown woman in Yathrib before the Hijrah and was still alive after the fall of Musaylimah, so her life links the secret meetings at Mina, the Prophet’s ﷺ Madinah and the first caliphate. Her name survives in the biographical dictionaries because those who wrote them recorded her as one of the Companions.
  2. A mother of Companions. Her son ʿAbdullah ibn Zayd is remembered as a narrator of the Prophet’s ﷺ ablution (wuḍūʾ), a report found in both Bukhārī and Muslim, and is reported to have been killed at the battle of al-Ḥarrah in 63 AH. Ḥabīb gave his life as an envoy, and ʿĀmmārah gave her the name by which she is known.
  3. A precedent for women’s service. Commentators on Bukhārī refer to the reports of women at Uḥud when they discuss women’s part in war: carrying water, nursing the wounded and, in an emergency like hers, defending the Prophet ﷺ himself.
  4. A source of knowledge. The ḥadīth she narrated about the angels’ blessing on the fasting shows that her place among the Companions was not only on the battlefield.

Qurʾān and Ḥadīth Behind Her Story

Several verses and ṣaḥīḥ reports explain what Nusaybah stood for. The verses are given in meaning, and the ḥadīths are cited by collection.

  1. Āl ʿImrān 3:139. “Do not lose heart nor grieve, for you will be the higher if you are believers.”

    This was revealed after the defeat at Uḥud, to steady the survivors.
  2. Āl ʿImrān 3:144." Muḥammad ﷺ is only a Messenger, and other messengers passed before him. If he dies or is killed, the believers must not turn back on their heels."

    This answers the rumour of his death at Uḥud.
  3. Āl ʿImrān 3:195. "Never will I allow to be lost the work of [any] worker among you, whether male or female; you are of one another."

    Scholars often cite this verse for women who took part in the early struggles.
  4. Āl ʿImrān 3:169–171.Verse 169: "And never think of those who have been killed in the cause of Allah as dead. Rather, they are alive with their Lord, receiving provision,"
    Verse 170: "Rejoicing in what Allah has bestowed upon them of His bounty, and they receive good tidings about those [to be martyred] after them who have not yet joined them—that there will be no fear concerning them, nor will they grieve."
    Verse 171: "They receive good tidings of favor from Allah and bounty and [of the fact] that Allah does not allow the reward of believers to be lost."

    These verses were revealed about the martyrs of Uḥud.
  5. al-Tawbah 9:71. Believing men and women are allies of one another: they command what is right, forbid what is wrong, pray and give zakāh.
  6. al-Aḥzāb 33:35. Allah lists the believing men and women together, and prepares for them forgiveness and a great reward.
  7. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Jihād. Anas (RA) saw ʿĀʾishah and Umm Sulaym at Uḥud carrying water skins on their backs and pouring water into the mouths of the wounded.
  8. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, Kitāb al-Jihād. Al-Rubayyiʿ bint Muʿawwidh (RA) said that the women went out with the Prophet ﷺ to give water, nurse the wounded and bring the dead and injured back to Madinah.
  9. Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī. After Uḥud, Qays ibn Abī Ḥāzim saw the hand of Ṭalḥah ibn ʿUbaydillāh paralysed, because it had shielded the Prophet ﷺ in that battle.

Lessons from Her Life

  1. Promises are tested later. A pledge made calmly by night shows its worth only when the day turns dangerous, and her life shows what it looks like to be the same person in both.
  2. Tenderness and resolve belong together. The same hands that bandaged a wound sent the wounded back to defend the Messenger ﷺ, with care and firmness in a single act.
  3. Aim for the highest company. When the Prophet ﷺ offered to pray for her, she did not ask for safety or reward in this world, but for companionship in Paradise.
  4. Grief can become purpose. Loss did not make her withdraw from the community; it moved her to serve it harder.
  5. Service takes many forms. She is remembered carrying water, nursing the wounded, fighting, raising sons and narrating ḥadīth, because each of these was what the moment required.
Nusaybah bint Kaʿb (RA) is remembered as a mother, a nurse, a pledge-keeper and a warrior, and above all as a woman who gave everything she had to defend the Messenger of Allah ﷺ.

Frequently Asked Questions

1. Where and when was the Battle of Uḥud fought?

It was fought at the foot of Mount Uḥud, a few kilometres north of Madinah, on a Saturday in Shawwāl, 3 AH (March 625 CE). About seven hundred Muslims faced a Qurayshī army of roughly three thousand.

2. Is “Lioness of Uḥud” her original title?

It is an honorific used by later writers. The early sources know her as Umm ʿĀmmārah, and they preserve the Prophet’s ﷺ own words about her courage.

3. Why do the accounts of her life differ in detail?

The Prophet’s ﷺ biographers wrote after his lifetime. Ibn Isḥāq (d. c. 150 AH), al-Wāqidī (d. 207 AH) and Ibn Saʿd (d. 230 AH) each collected reports from earlier narrators, and names, numbers and wording vary between them. Scholars therefore treat the broad outline as firm and the fine details with care.

4. Who was Asmāʾ bint ʿAmr?

She was a woman of the Banū Salimah, known as Umm Manīʿ. She and Nusaybah were the only two women at the second pledge of ʿAqabah.

5. Was she the first woman to fight in Islam?

The early sources do not call her the first. Other women also took part in the early campaigns, such as Ṣafiyyah bint ʿAbd al-Muṭṭalib, Umm Sulaym and al-Rubayyiʿ bint Muʿawwidh. Nusaybah is the one remembered above all for defending the Prophet ﷺ in person at Uḥud.

6. Is her story in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī or Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim?

The detailed account of her fighting comes mainly from Ibn Isḥāq (as transmitted by Ibn Hishām), al-Wāqidī and Ibn Saʿd. The two Ṣaḥīḥs do not tell her story themselves, but they confirm its setting: the Prophet ﷺ was left with only a few defenders, and women were present at Uḥud with water and care for the wounded.

7. What does Sūrah Āl ʿImrān say about Uḥud?

The passage from verse 121 to verse 179 is the Qurʾān’s longest treatment of a single battle. It opens with the Prophet ﷺ leaving home at dawn to post the believers at their stations (3:121) and closes with the sorting of the sincere believers from the hypocrites (3:179).

8. Why did Yamāmah lead to the collection of the Qurʾān?

So many reciters of the Qurʾān fell in that battle that ʿUmar (RA) urged Abū Bakr (RA) to have the revelation gathered in one volume. Zayd ibn Thābit (RA) tells the story in Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī, and he was the man chosen for the task.

9. Who were the Ansar?

The Ansar (“helpers”) were the Muslims of Madinah, from the tribes of Aws and Khazraj, who welcomed the Prophet ﷺ and the emigrants from Mecca. Nusaybah belonged to the Khazraj.

10. Which sources should I read first?

Ibn Saʿd’s Ṭabaqāt has her own biographical entry, and Ibn Hishām’s Sīrah and al-Wāqidī’s Maghāzī narrate Uḥud in detail. For readers of English, Martin Lings’s biography of the Prophet ﷺ is a good starting point.
